FoundationsP01: what a complex number is, and how it moves

1

What is i?

Multiplying by −1 flips a number to the other side of zero: a half-turn. Multiplying by i is half of that, a quarter-turn of 90°. Two quarter-turns make a half-turn, which is why i times i equals −1.

2

The complex plane

A complex number has two parts, so it needs two directions: the real part runs left and right, the imaginary part runs up and down. Every complex number is one point on this plane. Real numbers live on the horizontal axis; purely imaginary numbers live on the vertical one.

3

Addition

Adding means making one number's journey, then the other's from wherever you stopped. Left–right steps combine with left–right steps, up–down with up–down. The two directions never interfere.

4

Conjugate

The conjugate keeps the left–right position and swaps up for down: a mirror image in the real axis. The negative is a different move. It flips both parts, sending the point straight through the origin to the opposite side.

5

Modulus

The modulus is the straight-line distance from the origin. The left–right and up–down parts form a right-angled triangle, so the distance is its longest side. Distance alone does not pin a number down: a whole circle of points share it.

6

Argument

The argument is the direction a point faces, measured as a turn from the positive real axis. Anticlockwise turns are positive, clockwise turns negative. Many different turns end facing the same way; the principal argument is the one between −π (not included) and π (included).

7

Multiplication

Multiplying by a complex number does two things at once: it stretches the distance from the origin and turns the direction. Distances multiply, angles add. Multiplying by i is the special case with no stretch and a quarter-turn.

8

Two coordinate systems

One point, two sets of directions. "Go right, then up" is rectangular form. "Face this way, then walk this far" is polar form. Neither is more correct: rectangular suits adding, polar suits multiplying.

9

Powers of i

Each extra power of i is another quarter-turn, so the values cycle: i, −1, −i, 1, then repeat. For a large power, count how many full laps of four fit, then look at the steps left over.

10

Reference angle

To find a direction, start with the small acute angle the point makes with the real axis: the reference angle. Then let the quadrant decide how that angle becomes the principal argument. One reference angle, four different directions.

Proofs and powersM01: why the rules hold, and what repeated multiplication does

11

Modulus of a product

When two complex numbers multiply, their distances from the origin multiply too. Multiplying by w scales every distance by the size of w, whichever direction w points.

12

Quotient subtracts angles

Division undoes multiplication. Multiplying by w adds its turn; dividing by w takes that turn away. The result faces the difference of the two angles, and its distance is the quotient of the two distances.

13

Conjugate reflects the argument

Reflecting in the real axis keeps the distance and reverses the turn: an anticlockwise angle becomes the same-sized clockwise angle. One boundary case needs care: a negative real number is its own reflection.

14

Powers as a spiral

Each higher power repeats the same stretch and the same turn. The turns are equal, but the distance changes by the same factor every time, so the points spiral outward, run round a circle, or spiral inward.

15

Negative exponents

A negative power undoes repeated multiplication: shrink instead of stretch, turn back instead of forward. The points spiral in towards the origin. A negative exponent does not mean a negative answer.

16

When does a power land on an axis?

Each power adds the same turn. A power is real when the total turn is a whole number of half-turns, and purely imaginary when it lands exactly between them. Step through to see which power gets there first, and why nothing smaller can.
